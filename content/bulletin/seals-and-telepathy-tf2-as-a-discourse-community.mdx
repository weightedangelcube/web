What makes a piece of media timeless? Oftentimes, this is dictated by the
response said media garners from an audience. For instance, video game Team
Fortress 2 has been all but abandoned content-wise since its 10-year anniversary
in 2017; however, it continues to rank within the top 20 games on Steam by
concurrent player count (SteamDB, 2026). What, then, allows Team Fortress 2 to
continue to be so popular into the present day? Drawing from the criterion of a
discourse community by Swales (2016), this essay will use the definition of a
discourse community to prove that Team Fortress 2 is well-liked to this day
because it takes the role of a system of communication where its players author
meaning and create belonging, which is valued over winning.

Team Fortress 2 meets Swales’s criteria for a focal discourse community: an
“association of some kind that reaches across a region, a nation, and
internationally” (2016, p. 6). Players from across the world collaborate within
teams; being additionally free-to-play, it allows for a wide variety of
individuals to participate in the game. Swales indicates that discourse
communities must have mechanisms of intercommunication amongst members and
utilizes genres of communication in doing so. In Team Fortress 2, traditional
genres are found in taunts, which are animations players may select for their
character to perform; and voice commands, which play specific voice lines when
activated—to alert a nearby player that one is in need of assistance, for
example. This creates a small, fixed vocabulary that players rely upon to
communicate with each other. Swales also notes that discourse communities use
distinct terminology to communicate with one another, known as lexis. An example
of such lexis includes the “Über,” which the Medic charges by healing players;
when activated, it grants invulnerability for a period of time. Most of this
vocabulary is opaque to outsiders and new players; those who have played for a
long time know this language well.

What is most striking about Team Fortress 2's gameplay, however, is the
communication that is implied and completely invisible. This is best interpreted
through Swales' newer criterion for discourse communities: he notes that “a
[discourse community] develops a set of ‘silential relations’” (2016, p. 9). An
example of this wordless behaviour can be found in this clip of gameplay
footage, uploaded to Reddit: in Player Destruction map Selbyen, players kill
those on the enemy team to obtain buckets of fish, which they deliver to a seal
on the map for points. This seems to have gone awry, however: in the video,
players on both team suddenly gather and begin dancing around the seal as it
makes its journey from the centre of the map into the sea (FireMuffin303, 2023).
After the seal returns to the ocean, both parties resume fighting as if nothing
had happened. This event was not brought forward by an individual outright
stating, “there’s a seal, let’s dance”; rather, the only direct communication we
see between players consists simply of “seal,” which constitutes the
aforementioned silential relation: one word is enough to inform all players of
the meaning in conveyance. Participating in the dance, then, is performing
membership as in Gee’s definition of Discourse: enacting behaviour composed of
actions that present recognizable identities to others (2013). In this moment,
individuals are connected by a wordless, mutual understanding. This creates a
sense of belonging amongst players—notably not due to any aspect of the game
that could have enforced such behaviour, but due to the own volition of the
community itself. In other multiplayer video games such as Valorant, players do
not have the ability to author these interactions. Communication is limited to
that which is related to the objective, and therefore there is no community or
sense of belonging formed within matches. This makes Team Fortress 2 distinct in
the way that it allows for Discourse within its gameplay.

Players of Team Fortress 2 value the gameplay of the match more than its
outcome. Cook notes that the nature of casual matchmaking usually results in the
match being a stalemate for most of the game (2024). During this time, players
are usually off pursuing their own various goals rather than focused on the team
objective: engaging in wordless yet tauntful conversations with enemy players,
collaborating with teammates to perform humorous maneuvers, or having one-on-one
battles with particularly annoying opponents. According to Cook, most of the
enjoyment in Team Fortress 2 is found in these individual interactions, rather
than victory: “[players] don’t want to stomp the other team in five minutes,
[they] want to have an excuse to run around for 20 minutes” (6:59). Evidently,
the actions observed in the Selbyen clip are contrary to the intended purpose of
the game: the primary objective of the Player Destruction game mode is achieved
when killing as many players as possible, yet we observe both sides collectively
suspending that objective and engaging in a common ceasefire. Cook continues to
state that the favourite gameplay moments of most individuals are interesting or
humorous small interactions that had happened between players, and that for most
of these moments, the outcome of the match was completely disjoint to their
enjoyment. In video games where players are not able to create this meaning, all
enjoyment that one could derive must be related to the objective and whether it
is realized, that is, winning or losing. Yet in Team Fortress 2, the enjoyment
found in the match comes from interactions like the seal event and other
Discourse that composes the gameplay of Team Fortress 2, so that it becomes
irrelevant who emerges victorious in the match. The meaning found in Team
Fortress 2, then, is authored by the players, and not received through the
gameplay.

Team Fortress 2 remains beloved seventeen years after release because its design
turns play itself into a form of communication, creating a discourse community
whose shared goal is not victory, but the small, improvised interactions during
matches. Discourse is performed wordlessly, forming a sense of community that
brings forth a sense of belonging to players. This Discourse, authored by the
players, brings more meaning to the game than the outcome of the gameplay
itself. By studying Team Fortress 2 as a discourse community, one observes that
when players are free to construct meaning within interactions in a video game,
a close-knit community is formed that remains timeless throughout the ages.
